Algorithm & Architecture

How the Plagiarism Checker Works

A straightforward, mathematical walkthrough of the Natural Language Processing (NLP) pipeline that transforms raw unstructured text into comparable vector spaces.

The 7-Step Detection Pipeline

From raw text inputs to final similarity percentage

01

Input Documents

The user enters or uploads two documents (Reference Document A and Suspect Document B) via text areas or plain .txt files.

02

Text Preprocessing

Both texts are converted entirely to lowercase, punctuation symbols are stripped, and whitespace variations are normalized for uniform lexical analysis.

03

Tokenization

The continuous strings of characters are segmented into discrete atomic units called tokens (individual words) using regex word boundaries (\b\w+\b).

04

Stop-Word Handling

Extremely frequent grammatical words with little semantic uniqueness (such as "the", "is", "at", "which", "on") are filtered out so they do not artificially inflate similarity.

05

TF-IDF Vectorization

Each remaining word is converted into a real-valued numerical weight using Term Frequency × Inverse Document Frequency, reflecting its importance within the document corpus.

06

Cosine Similarity

The system calculates the cosine of the angle between the two high-dimensional document vectors. This metric evaluates orientation rather than document length.

07

Result Generation

The raw cosine coefficient (ranging from 0.0 to 1.0) is multiplied by 100 to yield a clean percentage and categorized as Low, Moderate, or High similarity.

Foundation Concept

1. Natural Language Processing (NLP)

Natural Language Processing is a subfield of artificial intelligence and computer science focused on enabling computers to understand, interpret, and manipulate human language.

In plagiarism detection, NLP bridges the gap between unstructured text written by humans and mathematical structures that computers can evaluate using linear algebra.

Text → Tokens → Vectors → Mathematical Distance
Feature Extraction

2. What is TF-IDF?

TF-IDF stands for Term Frequency – Inverse Document Frequency. It quantifies how important a word is to a specific document relative to a collection of documents.

TF (Term Frequency): Measures how frequently a word appears in a single document.
IDF (Inverse Document Frequency): Diminishes the weight of terms that occur frequently across all documents.

TF(t, d) = Count(t in d) / Total_Words(d)
IDF(t, D) = ln[(1 + N) / (1 + df(t))] + 1
TF-IDF(t, d, D) = TF(t, d) × IDF(t, D)
Distance Metric

3. What is Cosine Similarity?

Cosine similarity measures the cosine of the angle between two non-zero vectors in an inner product space.

Unlike Euclidean distance (which is sensitive to document length differences), Cosine similarity measures vector direction. Two documents discussing the exact same topic will have vectors pointing in nearly the same direction, yielding a score near 1.0 (100%).

Cosine Similarity(θ) = (A • B) / (||A|| × ||B||)

Step-by-Step Viva Example: Comparing Two Sentences

Here is a concrete mathematical walkthrough showing exactly how the algorithm calculates similarity between two slightly paraphrased sentences during an exam or viva demonstration.

Sentence A (Original):
"Machine learning is fascinating and powerful."
Sentence B (Comparison):
"Deep learning is powerful and fascinating."

Step A: Tokenization & Stop-Word Removal

Stop words like "is" and "and" are removed. The combined vocabulary extracted from both sentences is: ["deep", "fascinating", "learning", "machine", "powerful"].

Step B: Normalized Vector Representation (Sample Weights)

Vocabulary Term Sentence A Vector (Ai) Sentence B Vector (Bi) Dot Product (Ai × Bi)
deep 0.0000 0.5774 0.0000
fascinating 0.5000 0.4082 0.2041
learning 0.5000 0.4082 0.2041
machine 0.7071 0.0000 0.0000
powerful 0.5000 0.4082 0.2041
Sum Σ ||A|| = 1.0 ||B|| = 1.0 Cosine Similarity ≈ 0.6123 (61.2%)
Interpretation: Because 3 out of 4 non-stop words ("fascinating", "learning", "powerful") match between the two sentences, the cosine vector angle is sharp, generating a high similarity score of ~61.2% despite word order shuffling.

System Limitations & Ethical Considerations

While TF-IDF and Cosine Similarity provide a powerful and mathematically objective metric for lexical overlap, the numerical score does not independently establish intentional academic dishonesty.

  • Common Domain Terminology: Technical essays within computer science frequently use identical terms (e.g. "relational database management system", "polymorphism in object-oriented programming") which elevate similarity without any unethical copying.
  • Properly Attributed Quotes: Authors who quote reference material verbatim and provide full bibliographic citations will still show high lexical similarity in algorithmic scans.
  • Synonym Replacement & Semantic Shift: Standard bag-of-words TF-IDF relies on exact root tokens. Thorough rewriting using synonyms without changing concepts requires deeper semantic transformer models (such as BERT or Sentence-Transformers).
  • Requirement of Human Review: The tool is designed to assist educators and students in identifying sections that warrant manual editorial inspection, not to render automated disciplinary judgments.
